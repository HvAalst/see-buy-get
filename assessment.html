Tracking devices for sensitive shipments

Your question, 2026-11-03: “what does it take to wire in iot devices? for tracking sensitive shipments (at extra cost and specialized settings for the users who demand it)”. Most of what is inside the system is there already. What is missing is a way for a device’s readings to reach a load, the settings and the price around it — and a few rules outside the code. Three decisions come first.

There already

What a device would build on

  • A cold load keeps the range it must be kept at. Each reading is judged by it; a reading outside marks the cold chain broken, and the shipper and the carrying fleet are told at once. A reading made with no signal keeps the time it was made. Today every reading is typed by the driver.
  • Positions come only from the driver’s phone on a shift; the fleet’s map reads them every 45 seconds.
  • A business’s and a fleet’s own systems are told each step of their loads, and work with their own keys.
  • Every photo and paper is fingerprinted; the consignment note is frozen at each signature.
Missing

1. A way in for a device’s readings

A device is paired to a load: the driver scans the device’s code at pickup, and it stays on that load until delivery. Its readings land on the load’s record — temperature, position, shock, light (the box was opened) and battery — each with the device’s own time, since a device keeps its readings while it has no signal. Each says it came from the device, never mixed with what the driver typed. Three ways in:

  • The maker’s own system passes the readings to us (Tive, Sensitech, Controlant, Roambee and the like). The easiest: a small translator per maker, and we never touch the radio.
  • Bluetooth loggers read by the Driver app at each handover: cheap, no SIM card, good for thin networks — sent on when there is a signal. Android only; an iPhone’s browser cannot read Bluetooth.
  • The truck’s own tracker or fridge unit (Teltonika, Carrier, Thermo King): the fleet connects its own. Best for full truckloads.
Missing

2. Settings, for those who ask for it

“Track with a device” as a step in Post a load: what to watch and the limits (the temperature range is there already; shock and opening are new), how often a reading is taken, and who is told — the bell and the phone’s notifications, never a text. A fleet says “We carry devices”, and a tracked load is offered only to such fleets.

Missing

3. The extra cost

Whoever posts the load pays, as for the load itself: a charge for the device and our fee, shown before it is posted. A device the shipper or the fleet owns costs only our fee: we read it, nothing more. A device we rent out means stock, a deposit and getting it back after every trip.

Outside the code

4. Rules that are not ours to write

  • Air: an airline accepts only trackers it has approved on board, and lithium-battery rules apply. To legal.html, with its sources, when the first version starts.
  • A device we supply: radio approval in each country, and a SIM card that keeps working across borders.
  • Privacy: a device’s positions are location data; the privacy pages say so once it is built.
  • Medicines: the main reason people buy trackers, and refused until L29 is settled.

The three decisions

T1Whose devices come first?

Why it matters
Devices we own are stock: bought, lent with a deposit, brought back after every trip (a return leg of its own, like the empties), approved for radio in every country, each with a SIM card. A device the shipper or the fleet already owns needs none of that.
Options
  • The shipper’s own device, read through its maker’s system.
  • The fleet’s truck tracker or fridge unit.
  • Devices we rent out.
  • All three at once.
Recommendation
The shipper’s own and the fleet’s first: no stock, no returns. Renting our own later, once a customer asks for it and how they come back is decided.

T2Who may switch it on?

Why it matters
Whoever posts a load pays for it, unless we only match the two sides. Tracking is a service on top, so its price is said before posting, like the platform fee a business already sees.
Options
  • Any business, as a paid option.
  • Only for some goods (cold, high-value, fragile, dangerous).
  • Only under a contract with a fleet.
Recommendation
Any business, paid — offered first where it matters most: cold, high-value and fragile goods.

T3Which maker first?

Why it matters
Each maker’s system says things its own way. Inside, we keep one general way of saying a reading, and a small translator per maker — open formats, not a maker’s product dressed as ours. A translator is only proven on real readings from a real customer.
Options
  • Whichever a first customer already uses.
  • The most widely sold one.
  • Bluetooth loggers first (no SIM card, no maker’s system).
Recommendation
Whichever a first real customer already uses. Do you know a shipper or a fleet that uses one?
Once answered

The first version

Pairing at pickup; readings on the load’s record, said as the device’s; alerts on the range already kept, and on shock and opening; a broken cold chain on the consignment note as a driver’s reading is now; the setting and its price in Post a load; “We carry devices” for fleets; the business’s own system told; one maker’s translator and the general format. No hardware bought. About the size of the cold-load work.

Never

What a device will not do here

  • Judge a claim: a reading stands beside a damage report as evidence; damage is reported, not judged.
  • Score how a driver drives (left out, by decision — below).
  • Pass as our product: every reading names the device and its maker.
  • Send a text: alerts go to the bell and the phone’s notifications.

What is still thin, area by area

What exists in each area is on progress.html. What is thin there, as it stands on 2026-11-03.

Deliveries

One plan a day, and parcels not planned

  • One plan per fleet per day, started at 07:00 unless the office says otherwise.
  • The AI adviser over the plan has never answered for real: it needs a key on the server.
  • Parcels are not planned at all: a bag goes when a driver claims it.
Routes

Straight lines, by default

  • Outside a fleet’s day plan, distance is a straight line times 1.35, at one speed.
  • No routing server is set up for production.
  • Parcel arrival times are optimistic by decision.
Real time

Nothing re-plans during the day

  • A late truck does not reshuffle the rest of its day.
  • A tracking page says a parcel or a load is running late, but keeps the planned hour: nothing estimates it again from where the truck is.
  • The console and a fleet office have a live connection; everybody on a phone is on push (your answer, 2026-10-25).
Many places

Nothing above one fleet

  • No view or plan across fleets and hubs together. The old control tower was built for an overseas forwarder and is parked.
  • No timetable between hubs.

Inbound supply flows

D1–D14 were answered on 2026-10-25 (“Yes, A then B”, every recommendation as written) and are built: reading A — a business sees and receives what is coming to its own places — and reading B’s first step — a hub takes stores’ parcels in bulk — with the hub’s fees, goods from abroad, the empties and refused goods. Each is a row on progress.html. What is still open:

Your decision

Reading C: stock kept for a store

A store keeps its stock at a hub, and orders are picked from it. This is warehousing: storage, stock records, picking, and liability for goods at rest. The recommendation stands (D1, D3): decide it as a business question, on its own — and if ever, only as a service a hub declares and offers (its space, its price per unit per day, its terms), never one the network runs.

Thin

Reading D: gathering from the field

Farmers deposit at a co-op until a truckload is reached: built thinly as consolidation lots, which survive a restart. The co-op settles with its farmers off the platform.

Not built

A supplier on its own transport

A receiver sees what is coming only where it travels on our notes. A supplier that delivers with its own truck has no note with us. The recommendation (D4, D6): an expected arrival of the receiver’s own, pointing at no load, sent in by the same sheet reader as fleet orders or by the API — typed by hand last. A supplier is invited, never texted.

Later

Bookable slots at a dock

A hub declares its receiving days, hours and doors, and a store books a day. A time at a door is not booked: two carriers coming to one dock at 08:00 do not know about each other. The recommendation (D8): bookable slots once a hub or a dock actually queues trucks; a no-show clock starts from the booking.

Your decision, when it arises

People of a receiving place

A receiving place’s people may not be members of the business that ordered. Decide whether a place can have people of its own before staff at a place are built (D14). And a business group seeing its members’ arrivals: only when a real group asks (D7).

What we would not do in any reading: handle payment between businesses, work out customs duty, classify goods for customs, or keep a supplier’s prices.

Centralized logistics data

Connecting warehouse, transport and accounting systems and field data into one view, with nothing re-keyed. Inside SeeBuyGet the data is one. Built and on progress.html: the records in Postgres, lots that survive a restart, webhooks for every party (P1), an API for businesses and fleets (P2), a shipper’s own GS1 code on every package label, and the live connection (P4). Planning across sites and shifts

The six steps of 2026-10-16 and 17 — a fleet’s sites, how the vehicles were used, shifts at a site, loading bays and hours, a site short of trucks, a shipper’s own loading place — and your four answers are built, each a row on progress.html. Open:

Partial

Bays shared between carriers

A shipper’s bays are counted for each fleet’s own trucks only. Two carriers planning trucks to the same dock at 08:00 do not know about each other. Bookable slots at a dock would fix it (inbound).

Partial

A shipper the plan cannot name

A shipper’s hours are read only when the plan knows who the shipper is: the business that placed the order, or the one that linked the contract. An order a fleet types in for a shipper that has not linked its contract is planned without them.

Partial

Waiting for a bay

Bays are handed out after the orders are placed on trucks. A wait that takes a truck past its shift is said, but the plan does not move orders to avoid it.

Not built

Trailers and equipment

Trailers as assets of their own, and equipment at a site (a forklift that only some bays have) booked with a loading.

Not built

A forecast

How many trucks and drivers next week may need, read from past orders. The plan works only from orders already placed.

Two copies

The mother OS

M1–M7, SSE and the gateway each have an engine built early that keeps its state in memory, is closed to all but staff outside a sandbox, and is called by no app. The product built its own where it needed to. Most “partly” rows on the compendium page are that.

Left out, by decision

Not open: decided against, so they are not built by accident.

  • The workshop’s own business — parts stock, mechanics’ time, workshop invoices and payments. A job card is kept as a photo; what it cost is between the fleet and its workshop (quotations, never payments).
  • Costs per km — fuel and upkeep per km. Not recorded.
  • Scoring how people drive — harsh braking and speeding from trackers is monitoring of people, close to the line we do not cross (no employment). A deliberate decision if it ever comes, not a feature. This holds for tracking devices too.
  • Workforce beyond drivers’ shifts — your answer, 2026-10-17: who starts where and on which shift; never pay, contracts or hours counted for pay, and no staff counts or shifts at hubs or shops.
  • Judging how vehicles were used — your answer, 2026-10-17: recorded facts only, counted and never judged; no flags, no costs.
  • Traffic, and the road itself — traffic needs a paid provider; the road is the driver’s, so a lane has two ends, never a drawn route.

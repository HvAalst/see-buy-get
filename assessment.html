Logistics flow coordination — where we stand

How good SeeBuyGet is at organising deliveries, routes and inbound supply flows in real time across many places and shift patterns. What is built, what is thin, and what is missing. The longest part is about inbound supply: the decisions to make before anything is built.

The short answer

We are strong at planning a fleet's day and at tracking who holds a parcel at every handover. Real-time handling is partial. Inbound supply flows, in the warehouse sense, are close to missing. What is missing there is decisions, not code.

Strong

Planning a fleet's day

Orders from contract shippers or a spreadsheet are planned onto trucks by weight, driver hours, delivery windows and trips chained together. What does not fit goes to partner fleets, then the open board. Shippers are told when an order is left out or running late.

Strong

Many places, one custody record

Shops, hubs, store docks and yards record every handover, so the network always knows who holds a parcel. Alarms fire for parcels never dropped off or never collected.

Strong

A driver's shift

Start and end of shift with a chosen vehicle, the 13-hour limit and 9-hour rest said in advance, a snooze for new offers, and each fleet on its own clock.

Partial

Real time

Positions from phones on shift, a map that updates every 45 seconds, push from a live server. Nothing re-plans during the day. A parcel's lateness was only measured after it arrived (now told as it happens, see the last section).

Partial

Routes

The order of stops is optimised. Distances are straight lines times 1.35 at one speed, except in a fleet's day plan, which can use real roads if the operator runs a routing server. No traffic.

Partial

Shift patterns

Driver shifts are handled. The day plan counted every active driver, not who works that day (now a roster). A fleet's sites now keep shift patterns, loading bays and opening hours, and the plan says how many drivers each shift needs (see “Planning across sites and shifts”). Shops have opening hours; hubs have none, and no hub or shop has staff shifts.

Missing

Inbound supply

No expected-arrival notices, no receiving appointments, no counting goods in against what was expected, no storage. A store's dock is outbound only, by design.

Missing

Coordination across the network

Planning happens per fleet, per day. Nobody plans the parcel network as a whole: bags move when a driver chooses to claim them, and there is no timetable between hubs.

Area by area

Each area with what exists, what is thin, and what would move it.

Deliveries Strong

What exists

  • Contract orders from the Business app, or a CSV or Excel sheet the fleet office uploads, with every refused line said in words.
  • A day plan worked out by ordinary math: weight, equipment, driver hours, delivery windows (planned late rather than refused), trips chained from the last door, orders split when heavier than any truck, how many trucks to hire.
  • Overflow to partner fleets first, then the open board. Trips go out one at a time as trucks become free.
  • Shippers told when an order is left out, planned outside its window, running late, or delivered.

What is thin

  • One plan per fleet per day, started at 07:00 unless the office says otherwise.
  • The AI adviser over the plan has never answered for real: it needs an API key on the server.
  • Parcels are not planned at all: a bag goes when a driver claims it.

Routes Partial

What exists

  • The order of stops on each trip is optimised, and trips are chained.
  • A fleet's day plan can use real road distances from a routing server the operator runs (open protocol, no Google).
  • Drivers report road hazards (low bridges, weight limits, closures). The fleet map shows them.
  • Turn-by-turn is the driver's own maps app, opened from ours.

What is thin

  • Everywhere else, distance is a straight line times 1.35, at one speed.
  • No routing server is set up for production.
  • No traffic, by decision: it needs a paid provider.
  • Parcel arrival times are optimistic by decision ("ETA realism" is parked).

Real time Partial

What exists

  • Positions come from drivers' phones during a shift. The fleet map updates every 45 seconds while it is open.
  • Push from a live server, an inbox in every app, email by link.
  • Drivers nudged about loads near their road as they drive.
  • Freight running late is told to the shipper on the carrier's own clock.

What is thin

  • Nothing re-plans during the day: a late truck does not reshuffle the rest of its day.
  • A tracking page keeps the planned hour even when the truck is late.
  • The app asks the server again on a timer. There is no live connection.

Many places Strong Missing above one fleet

What exists

  • Shops, hubs, store docks, yards and container depots, each recording every handover in one custody chain.
  • A shop's opening hours decide when a parcel will be ready to collect.
  • Carriers advertise space on runs that leave anyway (departures).

What is missing

  • No view or plan across fleets and hubs together. The old control tower was built for an overseas forwarder and is parked.
  • No timetable between hubs. Hubs have no opening hours and no receiving capacity.

Shift patterns Partial

What exists

  • A driver's shift: vehicle, hours on duty, the limit and the rest, told before they bite.
  • Each fleet's own time zone. A plan's start hour and day length set by the office.
  • Shops' weekly opening hours.

What is missing

  • Different start times within one fleet (an early and a late shift), and two drivers sharing one truck in a day. The plan has one start and one length for every truck.
  • Staff shifts at shops and hubs. Hub opening hours.

Inbound supply flows: the decisions before the build

"Inbound supply" means goods coming INTO a place from a supplier: what is expected, when it arrives, what is counted, what happens with what is missing or broken, and who holds it afterwards. None of that exists yet. Most of it touches rules already set (who owns goods, who pays, who is told, who may see what), so it has to be decided before it is built. Each decision below gives the question, why it matters here, the options and my recommendation. Nothing in this section is decided.

First: what "inbound" would mean for us

Four different businesses hide under one word. Each reading is a different product.

Reading A

A business receiving at its own sites

A builder's yard expects bricks and cement from its suppliers. The goods already travel on our consignment notes; the receiver has no screen for what is coming. Smallest step: no new custody, money or liability.

Reading B

A hub as a cross-dock

A supplier sends a pallet of 200 web orders to an SBG hub; the hub breaks it down into parcels for many receivers. The natural next step for the parcel network: bulk in, parcels out.

Reading C

Stock kept for a store

A store keeps its stock at a hub, and orders are picked from it. This is warehousing: storage, stock records, picking, liability for goods at rest. A different company's product unless decided otherwise.

Reading D

Gathering from the field

Farmers deposit at a co-op until a truckload is reached. Already built thinly as consolidation lots; the co-op settles with its farmers off the platform.

Scope — what we are

D1Which reading do we build, and in what order?

Why it matters
Each reading brings different custody, money and liability. Building "inbound" without choosing builds the largest one by accident.
Options
  • A only — the receiver's view of what is already on our notes.
  • A, then B — the receiver's view, then hubs that break bulk into parcels.
  • A, B and C — become a warehouse operator as well.
Recommendation
A, then B. Decide C on its own, later, as a business question (D3), not as a feature.

D2Do we hold the commercial order (purchase orders, lines, prices)?

Why it matters
Our rule is quotations, never payments: we never handle businesses' payments, and a price between outside parties is theirs. A purchase order with prices on our record pulls us into their dispute when goods are short.
Options
  • Hold the full purchase order.
  • Hold the logistics half: a reference both sides understand, the expected handling units and weights, the expected day. No prices.
  • Hold a reference only.
Recommendation
The logistics half. A reference, never a price.

D3Do we store goods, or only pass them through?

Why it matters
Today custody always has an end in view: a shop holds a parcel for collection with a free window and then a daily holding fee; a hub holds a bag for hours and is flagged when it holds it longer. Storage is custody with no end date, and brings liability for goods at rest (damage is reported, not judged, and a company pays only where it has stated cover), a fee per unit per day, stock records, and papers such as a warehouse licence that each company keeps itself.
Options
  • Pass-through only: custody time-limited and flagged when exceeded.
  • Storage as a service a hub declares (its space, its price per unit per day, its terms), the way a shop declares its shelf and price.
  • We run the warehousing ourselves.
Recommendation
Pass-through first. Storage only ever as something a hub declares and offers, never something the network runs.

Records — what is written down

D4What is the record of an expected arrival?

Why it matters
The consignment note is the source of truth for anything we carry. A supplier who uses its own transport has no note with us. Two records stating the same facts drift apart.
Options
  • Inbound is a view over loads whose destination is the receiver's site — nothing new is stored.
  • A new "expected arrival" record, the receiver's expectation, linked to zero or more loads (zero when the supplier delivers itself).
  • Both: build the view first, add the record when B needs it.
Recommendation
Both, in that order. The expected arrival never copies what the note says; it points at it.

D5What is counted when goods come in: handling units or item lines?

Why it matters
We know parcels, bags and loads (weight, packaging, pieces). We have no product data, and pallets and trolleys as things we track are an idea not yet built (the hub would declare its own carrier; capacity is derived, never typed).
Options
  • Handling units: pallets, cartons, pieces, as on the note.
  • Item lines: product codes and quantities.
  • Both.
Recommendation
Handling units. Counting items belongs to the receiver's own stock system, and needs product data we do not have and should not own.

D6How do expected arrivals reach us?

Why it matters
Suppliers rarely hold our app. The fleet office already uploads orders as CSV or Excel with every refused line said in words, and store systems already sign their requests to us.
Options
  • Typed by hand by the receiver.
  • The same sheet reader as fleet orders.
  • An API for systems.
Recommendation
The sheet reader and the API first; typing by hand last. A supplier is invited, never texted.

D7Who may see an expected arrival?

Why it matters
An identifier opens nothing; a tenant is not a role; staff do not read a store's records except on a report. A hub that sees a supplier's prices, or one supplier who sees another's deliveries, breaks all three.
Options
  • Supplier and receiver each see their own; a hub sees what is addressed to it, without prices; staff on a report.
  • Wider sharing within a business group.
Recommendation
The first. Decide groups only when a real group asks.

The place — where goods come in

D8Who says how much a place can receive, and when?

Why it matters
Participants declare and the network reads: a shop declares its shelf and bag bays, a hub its zones and sort fee. A hub has no opening hours, no doors and no receiving capacity today. A country sets its own office hours; a place should set its own receiving hours.
Options
  • No appointments: first come, first served.
  • Declared receiving hours and doors, which carriers and the day plan read (delivery windows already exist in the planner).
  • Bookable slots per door, with no-show rules.
Recommendation
Declared hours and doors first. Bookable slots once a hub actually queues trucks. A no-show clock starts from the booking, never from a fixed date (a lesson paid for once already).

D9What happens at the moment goods are received?

Why it matters
The delivery signature on the note is the handover. A damage claim report is frozen at handover, later findings are notes beside it, and the sender has a copy. Each signed note is frozen and hashed in the archive. A partial delivery must leave the rest still expected, and a finished one must stay finished.
Options
  • Refuse the delivery when anything is wrong.
  • Accept with exceptions noted at the handover: short, over, damaged, wrong goods, with photos, on the same note.
  • Accept everything and file a claim later.
Recommendation
Accept with exceptions noted at handover. A damage report follows the rule we already have. What is short stays expected until the receiver closes it.

D10Whose clock decides "today" and "late" at a receiving place?

Why it matters
A fleet has its own time zone; a delivery window is read on the carrier's clock even where the door is in another zone. A receiving place in another country than its supplier has a third clock.
Recommendation
The receiving place's clock decides its own day and lateness. One clock per answer.

D11What happens to pallets, crates and trolleys, and to refused goods?

Why it matters
A network bag is interchangeable and can stay wherever it was emptied. A bakery's trolley belongs to the bakery and must go home: a return leg with no revenue and a custody chain for something that is not a parcel. Returns of parcels are half built.
Recommendation
Decide load carriers before bookable receiving at hubs. Refused goods go back as a return with their own custody, never as a status on the original delivery.

Money, borders and telling

D12Who pays for receiving, handling and (if ever) storage?

Why it matters
Fees follow custody events: whoever did the work is paid for it. Hubs declare their own sort fee. Our sister companies sell to the public; between businesses the price is a quotation between them.
Recommendation
Receiving at a hub is a fee the hub declares per handling unit, charged to whoever booked the inbound. Between two outside businesses it stays between them.

D13What changes when goods come in from abroad?

Why it matters
The note already carries the Incoterm and the clearance state, and says who may claim. We never work out duty or classify goods for customs.
Recommendation
An expected arrival from abroad shows whether it has cleared, from the note. Nothing more is computed.

D14Who is told what, and where?

Why it matters
Nobody is texted about goods; a business reads its bell in the app (no push for a business yet); store systems hear by webhook; documents go by link. A receiving site's staff may not be members of the business that ordered.
Recommendation
The receiver's business inbox and its systems' webhook. Decide whether a "site" can have its own people before site staff are built.

The order to decide in

Some answers change the questions after them.

  1. D1 and D3 — which reading, and whether we ever store. Everything else depends on these two.
  2. D2 and D5 — what we write down about the goods: a reference and handling units, or more.
  3. D4, D7 and D14 — the record, who sees it, and who is told. With these, reading A can be built: a business sees what is coming to its sites.
  4. D8, D9 and D10 — the place: its hours and doors, the receiving moment, its clock. With these, reading B can start at one hub.
  5. D11, D12 and D13 — empties and returns, money, borders. Needed before B runs at more than one hub.

What we would not do in any reading: handle payment between businesses, compute customs duty, classify goods for customs, or keep a supplier's prices.

Built

Who drives on which day

On the plan board for a day, the office ticks who drives and saves. The plan counts those drivers instead of every active one and names them; where nobody has said, it says it counted everybody. A driver put on a day or taken off one is told, and sees their coming days under “Your fleet”.

  • A planning fact only: no hours, no pay, and anybody can still start a shift.
  • Still open, a decision of its own: different start times within one fleet, and two drivers sharing a truck in a day.

Built

A parcel running late is told

A parcel is running late when the network holds it on its way and the time it was promised to be ready is more than two hours behind it. The receiver and a person who sent it are told once, in the app; a store hears on its systems. The tracking page, the parcel's screen and the list say it too.

  • Never before it was handed in, and not once it has arrived.
  • The two hours is because the promise is optimistic by decision.

Centralized logistics data

Connecting warehouse, transport and accounting systems (WMS, TMS, ERP) and field data into one operational view, with no manual reconciliation. Assessed 2026-10-15. Inside SeeBuyGet the data is already one; with other companies' systems it is far.

Near

One record per thing, inside

One server holds every parcel, load, run, fleet, driver, shop, hub and money entry, and every app reads those same records — our own apps need no reconciliation. Every company and person has one identity that ties their records together.

Near

Field data joins as it happens

Phone positions on shift, scans at counters and hubs, photos at drop-off and delivery, the waiting-time taps at gates, road reports and signatures on consignment notes all land on the same records the moment they happen.

Near

Money in the same system

Fees are booked in double-entry accounts from the handovers that earn them. Ledgers export as CSV; document bundles go to a customer's own storage (S3, WebDAV or a webhook). One tracking link answers for parcels, loads and booked space.

Partial

Other companies' systems

A store's, a business's and a fleet's own systems are told each step by signed messages, and a business's or a fleet's posts loads, sends orders in and takes loads with its own key (rounds 9–12, 8624da2). Books still leave as CSV that someone imports by hand. No standard formats, no warehouse system, no carrier feeds.

Partial

One view, live

The apps re-ask on a timer; there is no live connection. The console reads across everything, but there is no single control view; the digital twin built for one is fed and read by nothing. Parcels, loads, run bookings, lots and bags are separate kinds of record.

Done

1. Consolidation lots survive a restart

A cooperative's lots and its members' deposits were held in memory alone: a restart lost them. They are kept now, found by their code again after a restart, and a LOT number is never given twice.

Priorities

In this order. Each starts with the decisions named, which are yours.

P1Signed webhooks for every party, on every handover

What
Today only a store's systems hear, and only five events at the end of a journey. Every party with systems of its own — a store, a business, a fleet — would subscribe to what concerns it: taken in, loaded, at a hub, delivered, a problem reported, money settled. Signed, retried, in order, and readable back as a log, so their systems keep step with ours and nobody re-keys anything.
Decisions
  • Which events, and what each may carry for whom (a fleet must not learn a store's prices; a store must not learn a driver's position).
  • How long failed deliveries are retried, and who is told when a subscriber stays down.
Builds on
The store webhooks and their signing (vendor callbacks); the custody chain, which already records every handover as an event.

P2An API for businesses and fleets

What
A business's system posts loads and reads their status; a fleet's own transport system sends orders in and takes its day's plan out. The same keys stores already have (a key per system, revocable, sandbox and live), with what each key may do written on it.
Decisions
  • Which acts a key may do without a person (post a load: yes; accept a plan, or a price: probably not). Your answers, rounds 11–12: post, send in, take at its price; never accept a plan, agree a price, money or people. Built (8624da2).
  • Limits per key, and how a version of the API is retired. Your answer, round 12: a limit per key as stores have; a year's notice, every company with a live key told then and a month before. Built.
Builds on
The store API keys; the contract (756 operations, published); the order sheet reader, whose refusals in words would answer a system too.

P3Standards where they are cheap

What
An accounting export an ERP imports as it is — journal lines with accounts, not a ledger to retype — and GS1 serial shipping container codes (SSCC) on pallet labels, so a receiving warehouse scans our labels into its own system.
Decisions
  • Which accounting systems the first customers use, per country (that decides the format).
  • Pallet codes wait on the load-carrier decision (the hub declares its carrier; see D11 above).
Builds on
The double-entry books; the label service.

P4One live view

What
A live connection from the server to the console and the fleet office, instead of asking again on a timer; and the console's overview reading the digital twin, which is already fed by every parcel event and read by nothing.
Decisions
  • Who needs live (the console and fleet offices) and who is fine on push (everybody on a phone).

P5Reporting on its own copy

What
Now the records are in Postgres, reports can be asked of them. Asked of the live database they compete with the counter; a read-only copy (a replica, or a nightly export) keeps them apart.
Decisions
  • Who reads reports, and how fresh they must be (that decides replica or nightly).

P6Other carriers' feeds, and one kind of movement

What
A parser for carrier tracking feeds exists and nothing uses it: connect it where a load leaves our network on someone else's truck. And parcels, loads, run bookings, lots and bags read as one kind of movement for anything that looks across them ("movement unification", an old open item).
Decisions
  • Which carriers, if any, before testing — probably none.

Fleet and asset management

Keeping vehicles fit to go out, and knowing at once when one is not. Assessed 2026-10-15, built 2026-10-15 and 16. Before: vehicles were managed for their legal fitness (papers with expiry dates) and what they can carry (class, capacity, equipment), not their condition. Nothing kept a reading, a service schedule, a defect or a breakdown, and a grounded vehicle had no way back. Your words: “Build availability, checks, defects, service due dates and breakdowns. Leave out parts stock, mechanics' time, and workshop invoices or payments (quotations, never payments).”

Built

Built

1. Off the road, and back

A vehicle goes off the road with a reason (in the workshop, in for a service, after an accident, other), from a day until a day or until it is put back, and comes back by itself the day after. Every door reads it: a shift does not start in it, a plan for one of those days leaves it out, and the driver's list of vehicles says why. A vehicle grounded the old way can be put back.

Commit af6a94b.

Built

2. The odometer

The driver gives a reading as a shift starts and as it ends. One lower than the last is refused before the shift starts; at the end a reading never stands in the way — a slip is not kept, and the driver is told why. A jump bigger than a vehicle could drive (1,500 km a day) is kept and marked doubtful. The office records a new meter.

Commit e6dbe20.

Built

3. The daily check, and defects

A checklist for each class of vehicle: four items for a bike, nine for a truck; brakes, tyres and lights are safety-critical on all of them. A problem is said in words or a photo. A safety-critical one keeps the vehicle off the road until the office marks it fixed, or safe to drive as it is, always with a reason, and the driver is told which. A driver reports a problem at any time; a fleet can require the check before every shift.

Commit 1a8ae6a.

Built

4. Service due dates

Every so many km or months, whichever comes first: a truck every 15,000 km or 6 months, a bike every 3 months, or the office's own schedule for a vehicle. Counted from the last service, adding the km reading by reading, so a new meter does not lose the count. The office is told once when one comes due (a fortnight before, or a tenth of the km) and once when it is overdue. Each fleet chooses: overdue only warns (the default), or keeps the vehicle off the road. What was done is kept — a service, a repair, tyres, an inspection — with the day, the km, the workshop and a photo of its job card. Never a price or a part.

Commit edb2c29.

Built

5. A breakdown on the road

The driver reports it from the vehicle with where the phone is (or the shift's last known position) and a photo; the office can record one it heard of by telephone. The vehicle is off the road at once. The office is told with the position, and the shippers of what is aboard, or on its way to be collected, are told by their load, never by the plate. Today's planned trips for it are held, and the plan no longer sends a trip to any vehicle that is off the road. The office says how it ended: back on the road, or into the workshop until a day.

Built

6. The loads go on in another truck

Another driver of the same fleet takes everything over at the vehicle with the six digits on the other driver's phone (the pass counters already check, wrong tries counted). All of it moves, or none of it: room and what each load needs are asked first, and a run that has left goes on in the new truck. When the driver with the loads has gone home, the office moves them. Left out, by decision

Parts stock, mechanics' time, workshop invoices and payments. A job card is kept as a photo of what was done; what it cost is between the fleet and its workshop (quotations, never payments).

Out

Fuel and upkeep cost per km would help a fleet set its own price floor. Not recorded.

Out

Planning across sites and shifts

Drivers, loading places and vehicles, planned for a fleet that works from more than one place. Assessed 2026-10-16, built 2026-10-16 and 17. Your words: “Logistics resource optimization / Planning workforce, dock capacity, and equipment utilization efficiently across every site and shift. This is for fleets that operate from different locations.” Before: a fleet had one depot, typed when it signed up, and every truck in the day plan started there. A shift was only a driver's own start and end, never a pattern. Nothing said how much each vehicle was used.

Built, in the order you agreed

Built

1. A fleet's sites

The office adds its depots and yards on a map, says which vehicles and drivers work from each, and closes a site once nobody works from it. The depot the fleet signed up with is its first site. In the day plan each truck starts from its own site, and no more trucks leave a site than drivers start there. The roster can say a driver starts at another site for a day. Staff read the sites and change none of them.

A week at a time, for each vehicle, each site and the whole fleet: days out, days off the road, days standing idle, shifts and hours on shift, km driven where readings were given, loads delivered and their weight, how much of the distance ran loaded, and runs. Built

3. Shifts at a site
